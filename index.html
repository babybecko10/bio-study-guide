The big picture 🗺️

Before you drown in vocabulary, here's the whole story in 60 seconds.

Every living thing is made of tiny bags called cells. Each cell is basically a little factory wrapped in a fatty skin (the membrane). Inside, there's watery goo (cytoplasm), an instruction manual (DNA), and machines that read those instructions to build proteins (ribosomes). That's it. That's every cell.

There are two big flavors:

• • • • • •
Prokaryote

Simple. Small. No nucleus.
Bacteria & archaea.

Eukaryote

Complex. Big. Has a nucleus.
Animals, plants, fungi, protists.

🧠 The one mental model to keep

Cells are cities. The membrane is the city wall. The nucleus is city hall (holds the master plan). The mitochondria are power plants. The ribosomes are factories. The Golgi is the post office. When something is confusing, translate it back to the city.

A2.2.1

Cell theory 🧬

Cells as the basic structural unit of all living organisms.

The three statements of cell theory

  1. All living things are made of cells.
  2. The cell is the basic unit of life.
  3. All cells come from pre-existing cells. (No cell magically appears from goo.)

📖 "Theory" in daily vs. scientific language

In everyday speech, "theory" means a guess ("I have a theory about who ate my sandwich"). In science, a theory is way stronger — it's a well-supported explanation backed by huge amounts of evidence. Cell theory isn't a hunch; it's confirmed by ~200 years of microscopy.

Inductive vs. deductive reasoning (NOS)

Inductive reasoning

Many observations → general rule.

"Every organism I've looked at is made of cells → therefore ALL living things must be made of cells."

This is how cell theory was originally built.

Deductive reasoning

General rule → specific prediction.

"All living things are made of cells. This newly-discovered animal is alive → therefore it must be made of cells."

Uses the theory to predict.

A2.2.2

Microscopy skills 🔬

Two calculations you MUST be able to do on demand.

The one formula
M = I ÷ A
Magnification = Image size (drawing) ÷ Actual size

Calculation 1: Finding magnification

  1. Find the actual size from the scale bar (e.g. "50 μm").
  2. Measure the drawing size with a ruler (e.g. scale bar is 20 mm long).
  3. Convert units! Make them match. (20 mm = 20,000 μm)
  4. Divide: 20,000 ÷ 50 = ×400 magnification.

Calculation 2: Finding actual size (reverse)

If you know the magnification and the drawing size, rearrange:

Actual size = Image size ÷ Magnification

Example: A cell is drawn 30 mm across at ×600 magnification. Actual size = 30 mm ÷ 600 = 0.05 mm = 50 μm.

📏 Unit cheat-sheet — memorize this!

1 mm = 1,000 μm = 1,000,000 nm

Always convert BOTH measurements to the same unit before dividing. This is where most marks are lost.

A2.2.3

Developments in microscopy 🔭

How science invents better eyes.

Resolution vs. magnification — know the difference!

Magnification 🔍

How much bigger the image appears compared to the real object. (A magnifying glass makes an ant look huge.)

Resolution 🎯

The smallest distance between two things where you can still tell they're separate. Higher resolution = sharper detail. (You can zoom a blurry photo but not gain resolution!)

⚠️ Classic trap

High magnification without resolution = a big, blurry mess. Electron microscopes win because they have both high magnification AND high resolution.

Types of microscopes

TypeHow it worksPros & cons
Light microscope Uses visible light + glass lenses. ✅ Cheap, samples can be alive & in color.
❌ Low resolution (~200 nm limit).
Electron microscope (EM) Fires electrons instead of light. Much shorter wavelength → much better resolution. ✅ Insanely high magnification & resolution — can see organelles clearly.
❌ Expensive. Sample must be dead. Black & white only.

Advanced light techniques

Fluorescent stains 💡

Special dyes that glow under UV light. Benefit: lets you see specific structures in specific colors — perfect for spotting one type of protein or organelle.

Immunofluorescence 🎨

Antibodies are attached to a fluorescent dye. Because antibodies are super specific, they only bind to ONE type of protein. Result: whatever protein you're studying lights up in the microscope — everything else stays dark.

Target protein 🎯 ← Antibody 🔗 ← Fluorescent dye ✨
→ Structure lights up under UV → you see it clearly!

Advanced electron techniques

Freeze-fracture ❄️

  1. Freeze the sample super fast.
  2. Crack it open along the weakest layer — which is the middle of the membrane.
  3. Coat with metal & image.
  4. Result: you literally see the proteins EMBEDDED in the membrane. This is how we know membranes have integral proteins scattered through them.

Cryogenic electron microscopy (Cryo-EM) 🧊

  1. Freeze the sample so cold that atoms stop wobbling.
  2. Fire electrons at it.
  3. Result: you can image huge proteins & even viruses (like SARS-CoV-2!) at near-atomic detail.

A2.2.5

Prokaryotic cells 🦠

The simple ones. Bacteria & archaea. Example organism: Bacillus or Staphylococcus.

Simplified prokaryote — memorize this layout!

The 5 must-know parts + their functions

StructureFunction
Cell wall Made of peptidoglycan. Provides structure & protection. Draw as two lines with the label going between them.
Plasma membrane The inner boundary. Made of phospholipids + proteins. Controls entry/exit. Label as the inner line of the cell wall.
Cytoplasm Watery interior where metabolic reactions happen.
Nucleoid DNA The main chromosome. It's a single circular loop of DNA, floating free in a region called the nucleoid. Extra small loops = plasmids.

🎯 What does "naked DNA" mean?

Naked = the DNA is not wrapped around any proteins (like histones). It's bare — just the DNA molecule sitting free in the nucleoid region. Eukaryotic DNA, by contrast, is wound tightly around histone proteins.

⚠️ Common exam trap

Prokaryotes do not have mitochondria, ER, Golgi, or a nucleus. If you see any of those, it's a eukaryote. The nucleoid region is NOT the same thing as a nucleus — it's just a zone where the DNA hangs out, no membrane around it.

Eukaryotic cells 🏛️

The complex ones — full of specialized "rooms" called organelles.

An organelle = a specialized structure inside a cell that performs a specific job. Just like organs in a body.

🧨

Contain digestive enzymes. Break down waste, food, or old organelles.

Gives shape & moves things. Microtubules (railway tracks) + microfilaments (tension).

Reading & drawing micrographs 🖼️

Identifying cells and structures in electron micrographs — nearly guaranteed exam question.

Is it prokaryote, plant, or animal?

  • Prokaryote: tiny, no visible organelles, has a cell wall, DNA looks like a squiggle (nucleoid).
  • Plant cell: has a cell wall (straight edge, boxy shape), large central vacuole, chloroplasts (oval green structures).
  • Animal cell: irregular/round shape, no cell wall, no chloroplasts, no big vacuole. Just organelles.

Chloroplast

Oval, contains stacks of thylakoids.

Membrane sheets with dots (ribosomes) on them.

Smooth ER

Membrane tubes without dots.

Vesicle

Small membrane-bound bubble.

Drawing tips ✏️

  • Draw big. Small drawings lose marks. Fill the space you're given.
  • Use clean, unbroken lines. No sketchy hairy lines. One clean stroke.
  • No shading. Just outlines.
  • Label with a straight ruled line that TOUCHES the structure. Empty line = no mark.
  • Annotate = label + brief function. If a question says "annotate," don't just label.
  • Include a scale bar if you're reproducing the micrograph.
  • Draw the cell wall as TWO lines and label between them.
B2.1.1, B2.1.4, B2.1.9, B2.1.10

Membrane structure — the fluid mosaic 🧱

A wobbly phospholipid sandwich full of proteins.

Phospholipids — the bricks

A phospholipid is amphipathic — it has BOTH a polar and nonpolar end:

One phospholipid = polar head + 2 nonpolar fatty acid tails

When you drop them in water, they self-assemble into a bilayer — heads face out (toward water inside AND outside the cell), tails hide in the middle.

Where do you find lipid bilayers?

  • Plasma membrane (outer boundary of every cell)
  • Nuclear envelope (double membrane around the nucleus)
  • Membranes around all organelles (mitochondria, ER, Golgi, vesicles, etc.)
  • Inside the mitochondrion (the cristae folds)
  • Inside the chloroplast (thylakoid membranes)

The fluid mosaic model 🎨

The membrane is fluid (molecules drift around like a liquid) and mosaic (a mix of different molecules).

You must be able to draw this. Required labels: phospholipids (with head + tails), integral proteins, peripheral proteins, glycoprotein, cholesterol, hydrophilic head, hydrophobic tails.

Integral vs. peripheral proteins

Integral proteins

Embedded within the bilayer — often going all the way through. Have hydrophobic amino acids in the middle (so they stay put in the fatty core) and hydrophilic ones sticking out into the water.

Peripheral proteins

Attached to the surface only — never crossing the membrane. Anchored by weak interactions with the polar heads or with integral proteins.

The 6 functions of membrane proteins — "CRATER"

CRATER
LetterFunctionExample
CConnect (intercellular joinings)Proteins that link neighbouring cells to form tissues.
RRecognize (cell-cell recognition)Glycoproteins that identify "self" vs. invader.
AAnchor (attachment)Proteins that anchor the cell to the cytoskeleton or extracellular matrix.
TTransport (active or passive)Channels & pumps that move ions/molecules across.
EEnzymatic activityMembrane enzymes catalyze reactions right at the surface.

Glycoproteins & glycolipids 🎗️

Glycoproteins = protein with a sugar (carbohydrate) chain attached. Glycolipids = lipid with a sugar chain attached. Both have their sugar chains sticking OUT of the cell (into the extracellular space).

They act as ID tags:

  • Cell recognition — your immune system uses these to tell "self" from "invader." Blood types (A, B, AB, O) are literally different sugar chains on your red blood cell glycoproteins.
  • Cell adhesion — help cells stick together to form tissues.
B2.1.2

The membrane as a barrier 🚧

Why the membrane blocks some things and lets others pass.

🎯 Primary function of the cell membrane

To act as a selective barrier between the inside of the cell and the outside — controlling which substances can enter or leave.

The aqueous solutions (water-based) exist on BOTH sides of the membrane:

  • Outside = extracellular fluid (e.g. blood plasma, tissue fluid)
  • Inside = cytoplasm

The membrane's fatty middle sits between these two watery worlds.

Why the hydrophobic core is a barrier

Middle of the membrane = nonpolar fatty acid tails. This region:

  • ✅ Allows small, nonpolar molecules through (they're chemically similar, so they mix)
  • ❌ Blocks large molecules (too bulky to squeeze through)
  • ❌ Blocks polar/hydrophilic molecules like water and glucose (they don't want to enter a nonpolar zone)
  • ❌ Blocks ions like Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻ (charged particles are repelled by the nonpolar core)

This is why the membrane is selectively permeable — the physics of the phospholipid bilayer naturally filters what can & can't pass. Everything else needs a protein's help.

B2.1.3

Simple diffusion 🌫️

The most basic way stuff gets across.

Simple diffusion = the passive net movement of particles from an area of high concentration to an area of low concentration, directly through the phospholipid bilayer. No protein, no energy needed.

What affects the rate of diffusion?

FactorEffect
Concentration gradientSteeper gradient → faster diffusion. No gradient → no net movement.
Particle sizeSmaller = faster. Big molecules can't squeeze through the bilayer.
Polarity / chargeNonpolar molecules cross easily. Polar or charged particles are blocked.
TemperatureHigher temp → particles move faster → faster diffusion.
Surface areaMore membrane area → more room to cross → faster diffusion.

Two biological examples 🌬️

Oxygen (O₂) into cells

O₂ is small & nonpolar. Cells use it up in respiration, so its concentration inside is always LOW. O₂ diffuses IN from the blood (high concentration) across the membrane.

Carbon dioxide (CO₂) out of cells

CO₂ is a waste product of respiration, so its concentration inside is always HIGH. It diffuses OUT of the cell into the blood.

Like a ball rolling down a hill — diffusion always goes DOWN the gradient
B2.1.5

Osmosis & aquaporins 💧

The special case of water crossing membranes.

Osmosis = the passive movement of water molecules through a selectively permeable membrane, from an area of low solute concentration to an area of high solute concentration.

Why water needs help — aquaporins

Water is a small but polar molecule, so it can't cross the nonpolar bilayer easily. Cells use special channel proteins called aquaporins.

💦 Aquaporin structure & function

Aquaporins are integral membrane proteins that form a channel shaped so only water molecules fit through. They're extremely selective — they let water pass but block ions and other solutes. Cells with lots of water transport (like kidney cells) have LOTS of aquaporins.

The magic phrase

Solute sucks. 💥

The side with more solute pulls water toward it. Think of it as a tug-of-war — whoever has more solute wins the water.

The tonicity vocabulary

TermMeaningWhat happens to a cell placed in it?
Hypertonic Higher solute concentration (outside > inside) Water leaves the cell → cell shrinks (animal cells crenate; plant cells plasmolyze)
Hypotonic Lower solute concentration (outside < inside) Water enters cell → cell swells. Animal cells may burst (lysis). Plant cells become turgid (good!).
Isotonic Same concentration on both sides No net movement — cell stays normal.

⚠️ Predicting water direction

Ask yourself: which side has MORE solute? Water moves toward that side. Doesn't matter which side you're "in" — water always follows the solute.

B2.1.6

Channel proteins — facilitated diffusion 🚪

How polar/charged molecules cross when the bilayer blocks them.

Facilitated diffusion = passive movement of particles from high → low concentration, THROUGH a channel protein. Still no ATP required — the protein just provides a hydrophilic tunnel.

Channel protein structure

  • Integral protein that spans the entire membrane.
  • Has a hydrophilic tunnel in the middle — this lets polar/charged particles pass without touching the fatty core.
  • Each channel is specific to one type of ion or molecule (its shape only fits certain particles).

Specificity of channels for ions

The cell controls what enters/leaves by choosing which channels to make. Want more sodium in? Build more Na⁺ channels. Don't want potassium out? Don't make K⁺ channels. This is how cells achieve selective permeability.

Types of gates on channel proteins

Many channels are gated — they can open or close depending on conditions:

⚡

Voltage-gated

Open/close in response to changes in electrical charge across the membrane. Key in nerve cells.

🔗

Ligand-gated

Open when a specific molecule (a "ligand") binds to them — like a neurotransmitter.

🤲

Mechanically-gated

Open in response to physical pressure or stretching.

💡

Light-gated

Open in response to light — used in retinal cells.

Example: potassium (K⁺) channels

K⁺ ions are too charged & polar to cross the bilayer. K⁺ channels have a hydrophilic pore that lets K⁺ specifically pass through — down its concentration gradient. No ATP needed.

B2.1.7

Pump proteins & active transport ⚡

Moving molecules the WRONG way — uphill against the gradient.

Active transport = movement of molecules from LOW → HIGH concentration (against the gradient) through a pump protein, requiring energy from ATP.

How pump proteins work

  1. Molecule binds to a specific site on the pump (specific to certain molecules only).
  2. ATP donates energy → causes a conformational change (the pump's shape shifts).
  3. The shape change moves the molecule to the other side and releases it.
  4. Pump returns to original shape, ready for another cycle.
Pushing a ball uphill — requires energy!

Facilitated diffusion vs. active transport

Facilitated diffusionActive transport
Protein typeChannelPump
DirectionDOWN gradient (high → low)UP gradient (low → high)
Energy?❌ No ATP✅ Requires ATP
SpecificityYes (each channel is specific)Yes (each pump is specific)

Example: absorbing minerals in plant roots 🌱

Plants need mineral ions (like nitrate NO₃⁻) from the soil, but those ions are usually at lower concentration in the soil than inside the root cells. Root cells use active transport pumps to bring nitrate IN against the gradient, powered by ATP.

B2.1.8

Selectivity in membrane permeability 🎛️

How cells control exactly what gets in.

Selective permeability = the membrane's ability to allow some substances through while blocking others.

How the membrane achieves selectivity

  • Channels & pumps are specific to particular molecules — a Na⁺ channel only lets Na⁺ through, not K⁺.
  • The cell chooses which channels & pumps to build.
  • By adjusting the number and type of proteins, the cell can precisely control what enters/leaves.

Simple diffusion is NOT selective

Simple diffusion doesn't pick and choose — it just depends on:

  • Size of the particle
  • Polarity or charge
  • Concentration gradient

Any small nonpolar molecule can pass. The cell can't stop it. Real selectivity comes from channels and pumps, not from the bilayer itself.

B2.2.1

Organelles as discrete subunits 🏛️

Why the eukaryotic cell is divided into rooms.

An organelle is a discrete (separate, distinct) structure within a cell that is adapted to perform a specific function.

Grouping organelles by membranes

TypeExamples
Solid (no membrane) Ribosomes
Single-membrane Endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, vesicles, lysosomes, vacuoles
Double-membrane Nucleus, mitochondrion, chloroplast

What is NOT an organelle

These are structural or general parts of the cell, not organelles:

  • Cell wall — structural boundary, not an organelle
  • Cytoskeleton — network of protein filaments, structural role
  • Cytoplasm — the medium in which organelles sit, not a specialized structure

They don't count because they're either (a) general/structural rather than performing a specific discrete function, or (b) they're the environment the organelles live in.

B2.2.2

Separating the nucleus from cytoplasm 🧬

Why eukaryotes lock their DNA away.

The main benefit

Keeping the DNA in the nucleus (separated from the cytoplasm) lets eukaryotes control the timing and processing of gene expression.

Comparing prokaryotes and eukaryotes

ProkaryotesEukaryotes
Where does transcription (DNA → RNA) happen? Cytoplasm (there's no nucleus) Inside the nucleus
Where does translation (RNA → protein) happen? Cytoplasm Cytoplasm (at ribosomes)
Timing Translation starts while transcription is still going! Transcription must FINISH before translation begins.
Post-transcriptional modification? ❌ Not possible ✅ Yes — mRNA can be edited before leaving the nucleus

Why post-transcriptional modification isn't possible in prokaryotes

In prokaryotes, the DNA is right there in the cytoplasm with the ribosomes. As soon as an mRNA molecule starts being made, ribosomes latch on and start translating it immediately. There's no time or space to modify the mRNA first.

In eukaryotes, mRNA is made in the nucleus, then edited (things like splicing out introns), and THEN sent out through nuclear pores to reach the ribosomes. This is only possible because the two compartments are separate.

DNA → transcription → raw mRNA → processing → edited mRNA → translation → Protein
B2.2.3

Advantages of compartmentalization 🏘️

Why bother having all those organelles?

⚡

Efficient metabolism

Enzymes and substrates can be concentrated in small spaces → reactions happen faster.

🧪

Local conditions

Each organelle can maintain its own optimal pH, ion concentration, etc. — different from the rest of the cell.

☠️

Toxic isolation

Dangerous chemicals kept safely locked away where they can't damage the cell.

🔀

Flexibility

Cell can change the number of each organelle based on need (e.g. more mitochondria = more energy).

The lysosome example 🧨

Lysosomes contain powerful digestive enzymes (like proteases and lipases). If these enzymes floated free in the cytoplasm, they would digest the cell itself!

By locking them inside a membrane-bound compartment (the lysosome), the cell can:

  • Safely store digestive enzymes without harm
  • Merge lysosomes with food vacuoles to digest food
  • Merge lysosomes with old/damaged organelles to recycle them
B2.3.1 – B2.3.4

Stem cells & differentiation 🌱

How one fertilized egg becomes 200+ cell types.

The story of you

  1. Sperm + egg → zygote (a single unspecialized cell)
  2. Zygote divides many times → blastocyst (hollow ball with inner cell mass)
  3. Stem cells detect morphogens (chemical gradients) telling them what to become
  4. They differentiate by expressing some genes and silencing others
  5. Result: muscle cells, neurons, red blood cells, skin cells, etc.

🎯 Morphogens & chemical gradients

Morphogens are signal molecules released from "source cells." Their concentration decreases with distance from the source. Cells at different distances see different concentrations, which turns on different genes → they develop into different tissue types. This is how a symmetrical embryo turns into a body with organs in specific places.

What is differentiation?

Differentiation = the process by which a cell becomes specialized for a particular function. It happens by gene expression — every cell has the same DNA, but only some genes are "switched on" in each cell type. A muscle cell has the exact same DNA as a neuron, but they use different genes.

Properties of stem cells

All stem cells have two superpowers:

  1. Self-renewal — can divide endlessly to make more copies of themselves.
  2. Potency — can differentiate into specialized cell types.

Types of stem cells — the potency ladder

TypeCan become…Example
Totipotent Anything — including placenta cells Zygote (days 0–4 after fertilization)
Pluripotent Any body cell (but NOT placenta) Inner cell mass of blastocyst
Multipotent A few related cell types Bone marrow → any type of blood cell

🤔 Why are pluripotent cells most common in early embryos?

Because the embryo needs to build EVERY tissue type of the body from scratch. Once tissues are formed, most stem cells become multipotent (only need to replace one tissue type). So pluripotent cells are only really needed early on.

Stem cell niches in adults

Even after development, some stem cells stick around in special microenvironments called niches:

🦴 Bone marrow niche

Contains hematopoietic stem cells. Multipotent — they produce all types of blood cells (red, white, platelets). Location: inside long bones like the femur.

💇 Hair follicle niche

Multipotent stem cells found in a "bulge" region of the hair follicle. Can regenerate skin and hair tissues. Big potential for medical treatments.

Cell size as an aspect of specialization 📏

Different jobs need very different sizes.

Human cells vary enormously in size — the egg cell is about 100,000× bigger by volume than the sperm cell! Each size is matched to the cell's job.

Cell typeSizeWhy?
Sperm cell 🏊 Very small (~30 μm³) Needs to swim fast to reach the egg. Minimal cytoplasm to reduce weight; long flagellum for propulsion.
Egg cell (ovum) 🥚 Huge (~4,000,000 μm³) Needs to store nutrients for the early embryo before implantation. Huge cytoplasm loaded with supplies.
Red blood cell 🩸 Small (~7.5 × 2 μm) Must squeeze through tiny capillaries (~8 μm diameter). Biconcave shape maximises surface area for O₂ exchange.
White blood cell 🛡️ Medium (10–21 μm) Lymphocytes (~10 μm) secrete antibodies. Macrophages (~21 μm) engulf whole pathogens — need to be big.
Neuron 🧠 Very long (up to 1 metre) but narrow (~10 μm diameter) Long axon carries electrical signals over long distances (e.g. from spine to foot). Narrow so signals travel fast.
Skeletal muscle fiber 💪 Long (2–3 cm), 10–100 μm diameter Length allows for greater contraction. Multiple nuclei needed to control the whole cell.

Surface area to volume ratio 📐

The single reason cells are so small.

Everything the cell needs (O₂, glucose) must cross the membrane. So surface area = how fast stuff can enter/leave.

Meanwhile, volume = how much material the cell has to feed. Bigger cell = more demand.

The problem: geometry hates you

When a cell gets bigger, volume grows faster than surface area. Imagine cube-shaped cells of different sizes:

Side lengthSA (6s²)Volume (s³)SA:Vol ratio
1616 : 1 😄
22483 : 1
496641.5 : 1
1060010000.6 : 1 😰

See? A cell that gets too big literally can't absorb enough nutrients to feed itself.

Why cells are limited by SA:V ratio

  • Materials that must ENTER the cell (O₂, nutrients) enter through the membrane → depend on surface area.
  • Waste that must LEAVE the cell (CO₂, metabolic waste) also crosses the membrane → depends on surface area.
  • The AMOUNT of these materials needed depends on how much cell there is → volume.
  • If SA:V drops too low, the cell can't exchange materials fast enough → dies.

Solutions cells use

  • Stay small and divide when they get too big
  • Become long & thin (like neurons) — high surface area, small volume
  • Grow folds/projections like microvilli on intestinal cells to increase surface area without much volume

🧠 One-line takeaway

Big cells starve. That's why we're multicellular, and why absorptive cells have microvilli.

Final prep

Exam-day tips 🎯

  • Draw big. Small drawings lose marks for clarity. Fill the space.
  • Label with lines that TOUCH the structure. A label line ending in empty space = no mark.
  • Give function AND structure when asked to "annotate." Just labelling loses marks.
  • Match the command term. "Outline" = short list. "Explain" = give reasons. "Compare & contrast" = similarities AND differences.
  • Convert units BEFORE dividing in magnification questions. Common mistake.
  • Watch the word "all." "All cells have ribosomes" ✅. "All cells have mitochondria" ❌ (prokaryotes don't).
  • Memorize these lists exactly: the 4 features common to all cells, MR H GREN, CRATER, the potency ladder.
  • Cell wall = draw two lines with the label between them. Same for the double membrane of nucleus/mitochondria.

⚠️ Classic exam traps to avoid

  • Saying prokaryotes have a nucleus (they don't — just a nucleoid REGION, no membrane)
  • Confusing hyper/hypotonic → remember "solute sucks"
  • Forgetting active transport needs ATP
  • Writing "diffuses through channel protein" for O₂ — that's simple diffusion, no protein needed!
  • Confusing rough ER (proteins) with smooth ER (lipids)
  • Mixing up magnification with resolution
  • Calling the cytoskeleton or cell wall an organelle (they're not)

Self-quiz 🎓

Click each question to reveal the answer. 25 questions covering the whole syllabus.

1. What are the three statements of cell theory?
Answer: (1) All living things are made of cells. (2) The cell is the basic unit of life. (3) All cells come from pre-existing cells.
2. What's the difference between "theory" in daily language vs. science?
Answer: In daily language, a "theory" is just a guess. In science, a theory is a well-supported explanation backed by extensive evidence — much stronger than a guess.
3. Distinguish inductive from deductive reasoning.
Inductive: many specific observations → general rule.
Deductive: general rule → specific prediction.
4. Formula for magnification? Formula for actual size?
Magnification = drawing size ÷ actual size.
Actual size = drawing size ÷ magnification.
Always convert units first (1 mm = 1,000 μm = 1,000,000 nm)!
5. What's the difference between magnification and resolution?
Magnification: how much bigger the image looks.
Resolution: the smallest distance at which you can still tell two things apart. High magnification without resolution = blurry mess.
6. Give one advantage and one disadvantage of the electron microscope vs. light microscope.
Advantage: Much higher magnification & resolution. Disadvantage: Sample must be dead; images are only in black & white; very expensive.
7. Outline immunofluorescence.
Answer: Fluorescent dyes are attached to antibodies. The antibodies bind specifically to one type of protein, which then glows under UV light — letting you see that specific structure in the cell.
8. Name the four features common to ALL cells.
Answer: DNA, cytoplasm, ribosomes, plasma membrane.
9. What does "naked" DNA mean?
Answer: The DNA is not wrapped around any proteins (like histones). In prokaryotes, DNA is naked; in eukaryotes, it's wrapped around histone proteins.
10. Compare prokaryotic and eukaryotic DNA.
Prokaryote: naked, circular, in nucleoid region.
Eukaryote: bound to histones, linear, inside nucleus.
11. What does the rough ER do and how is it different from smooth ER?
Rough ER: covered in ribosomes; makes proteins for export or membrane.
Smooth ER: no ribosomes; makes lipids and detoxifies drugs.
12. What are the 8 functions of life (MR H GREN)?
Metabolism, Response, Homeostasis, Growth, Reproduction, Excretion, Nutrition — plus Movement.
13. Describe how Paramecium performs the functions of life.
Answer: Uses cilia to swim (movement); detects chemicals (response); sweeps food into oral groove & digests in food vacuoles (nutrition & metabolism); contractile vacuoles pump out excess water (homeostasis); waste diffuses across membrane (excretion); reproduces by binary fission.
14. What's the cell wall of plants made of? Fungi?
Plants: cellulose. Fungi: chitin.
15. Describe features of RBCs, skeletal muscle fibers, aseptate fungal hyphae, and phloem sieve tubes that make them atypical.
Red blood cells: no nucleus (ejected during development so they fit through capillaries).
Skeletal muscle fibers: many nuclei (too long for one nucleus to control).
Aseptate fungal hyphae: many nuclei (same reason).
Phloem sieve tubes: no nucleus (removed to leave hollow tube for sap flow).
16. Why is the hydrophobic core of the bilayer a barrier?
Answer: The nonpolar hydrocarbon chains repel polar molecules (like water and glucose) and charged particles (ions). Large molecules also can't squeeze through. Only small nonpolar molecules can cross freely.
17. List the 6 functions of membrane proteins (CRATER).
Connect (intercellular joinings) · Recognize (cell-cell recognition) · Anchor (attachment) · Transport · Enzymatic activity · Receptors (signal transduction).
18. Compare integral and peripheral proteins.
Integral: embedded within the bilayer (often spanning it); anchored by hydrophobic amino acids in the fatty core.
Peripheral: attached only to the surface; do not cross the membrane.
19. Define osmosis.
Answer: The passive movement of water molecules through a selectively permeable membrane, from an area of low solute concentration to an area of high solute concentration.
20. If a red blood cell is placed in pure water, what happens?
Answer: Pure water is hypotonic. Water rushes IN by osmosis. RBC swells and eventually bursts (lysis).
21. List the 4 types of gated channel proteins.
Answer: Voltage-gated, ligand-gated, mechanically-gated, light-gated.
22. Compare facilitated diffusion and active transport.
Facilitated diffusion: passive, DOWN gradient, uses a channel, no ATP.
Active transport: uses a pump, UP gradient, requires ATP. Pump undergoes conformational (shape) change to move molecule.
23. Why can't prokaryotes do post-transcriptional modification?
Answer: Prokaryotes lack a nucleus, so DNA and ribosomes are in the same compartment (cytoplasm). Translation starts on the mRNA before transcription is even finished — no time or space for modification.
24. Give 3 advantages of compartmentalization + why lysosomes are a great example.
Advantages: (1) Enzymes and substrates concentrated → more efficient reactions. (2) Local conditions (pH, ion concentration) can be optimized. (3) Toxic substances isolated safely.
Lysosome example: contains digestive enzymes that would destroy the cell if released. The membrane keeps them safely contained.
25. Explain why cells are limited in size by SA:V ratio.
Answer: As cells grow, volume increases faster than surface area (V = s³ vs SA = 6s²). Eventually SA:V drops too low, and the cell can't absorb enough oxygen/nutrients or expel waste fast enough. So cells either stay small, divide, or develop shape adaptations (like microvilli) to increase surface area.
