The big picture 🗺️

Before you drown in vocabulary, here's the whole story in 60 seconds.

Every living thing is made of tiny bags called cells. Each cell is basically a little factory wrapped in a fatty skin (the membrane). Inside, there's watery goo (cytoplasm), an instruction manual (DNA), and machines that read those instructions to build proteins (ribosomes). That's it. That's every cell.

There are two big flavors:

• • • • • •
Prokaryote

Simple. Small. No nucleus.
Bacteria & archaea.

Eukaryote

Complex. Big. Has a nucleus.
Animals, plants, fungi, protists.

🧠 The one mental model to keep

Cells are cities. The membrane is the city wall. The nucleus is city hall (holds the master plan). The mitochondria are power plants. The ribosomes are factories. The Golgi is the post office. When something is confusing, translate it back to the city.

A2.2.1 – A2.2.3

Cell theory & microscopy 🔬

The rules everyone agrees on, and the tools that let us see the tiny stuff.

The three rules of cell theory

  1. All living things are made of cells.
  2. The cell is the basic unit of life.
  3. All cells come from pre-existing cells. (No cell magically appears from goo.)

💡 NOS: Inductive vs. deductive reasoning

Inductive = many observations → general rule. ("Every cell I've seen has DNA... so all cells probably have DNA.")

Deductive = general rule → specific prediction. ("All cells have DNA, so THIS newly-discovered animal has cells with DNA.")

Calculating magnification 🧮

This is one of the few actual math formulas in this whole unit. Memorize it:

Magnification =
Drawing ÷ Actual
(size of the image ÷ real size)

📏 Step-by-step

  1. Find the actual size from the scale bar (e.g. "50 μm").
  2. Measure the drawing size with a ruler (e.g. scale bar is 20 mm).
  3. Convert units! Make them match. (20 mm = 20,000 μm)
  4. Divide: 20,000 ÷ 50 = ×400 magnification.

Unit cheat-sheet: 1 mm = 1,000 μm = 1,000,000 nm

Types of microscopes

TypeHow it worksPros & cons
Light microscope Uses visible light + glass lenses. ✅ Cheap, samples can be alive & in color.
❌ Low resolution (can't see anything smaller than ~200 nm).
Electron microscope (EM) Fires electrons at the sample instead of light. ✅ Insanely high magnification & resolution.
❌ Expensive. Sample must be dead. Black & white only.
Immunofluorescence Antibodies that glow are attached to specific cell parts. ✅ You can literally light up specific organelles in different colors.
Cryogenic EM Freeze the sample, then EM it. ✅ Atoms hold still → you can image huge molecules like proteins & viruses.
Freeze-fracture Freeze, then snap the sample open along the membrane. ✅ Lets us peek at proteins inside the membrane.

A2.2.4

What ALL cells have 🌟

Every single cell on Earth — bacterium, moss, or elephant — shares these four things.

🧬

DNA

The genetic blueprint. Tells the cell what proteins to build.

💧

Cytoplasm

Water-based jelly where chemical reactions happen.

⚙️

Ribosomes

Tiny protein-building machines. Read DNA instructions.

🛡️

Plasma membrane

The fatty outer boundary. Controls what comes in & out.

Why does this matter? Because whenever the exam asks "what do prokaryotes and eukaryotes have in common?" — this list is the answer.

A2.2.5

Prokaryotic cells 🦠

The simple ones. Bacteria & archaea. No nucleus, no organelles.

Simplified prokaryote (like E. coli or Bacillus)

The 5 must-know parts

  • Cell wall — made of peptidoglycan in bacteria. Provides structure & protection. Draw it as two lines.
  • Plasma membrane — the inner boundary. Made of phospholipids + proteins.
  • Cytoplasm — the watery interior where reactions happen.
  • Naked, looped DNA — floats free in the nucleoid region (not a real organelle, just a zone). Sometimes there are extra small loops = plasmids.
  • 70S ribosomes — smaller than the 80S ones in eukaryotes. Draw as tiny dots.

⚠️ Common exam trap

Prokaryotes do not have mitochondria, ER, Golgi, or a nucleus. If you see any of those, it's a eukaryote. Also — the DNA is naked (no histones) and circular, not linear.

A2.2.6 & B2.2

Eukaryotic cells & their organelles 🏛️

The complex ones. Compartmentalized. Full of specialized rooms called organelles.

An organelle = a specialized structure inside a cell that performs a specific job. Just like organs in a body.

🚨 Not everything is an organelle

Structural parts like the cell wall, cytoskeleton, and cytoplasm are NOT organelles. Functional parts like the nucleus, mitochondria, ribosomes, vesicles, and plasma membrane ARE.

The organelle roster 🎭

🏛️

Nucleus

Function: stores DNA, controls the cell. Has a double membrane with pores for mRNA to exit.

🔋

Mitochondria

Function: aerobic respiration → makes ATP (energy). Double membrane; inner one folded into cristae.

🏭

Rough ER

Function: makes proteins for export. Covered in ribosomes → looks "rough."

🧪

Smooth ER

Function: makes lipids, detoxifies drugs & alcohol. No ribosomes.

📮

Golgi apparatus

Function: modifies & packages proteins into vesicles for shipping. Stack of flat sacs.

📦

Vesicles

Function: transport materials around the cell. Little membrane bubbles.

🧨

Lysosomes

Function: contain digestive enzymes. Break down waste & old organelles.

🌿

Chloroplasts (plants only)

Function: photosynthesis. Double membrane, contain green chlorophyll.

🕸️

Cytoskeleton

Function: gives shape & moves things. Microtubules (railways) + microfilaments (tension).

⚙️

80S Ribosomes

Function: protein synthesis. Bigger than prokaryote ribosomes. Free-floating OR bound to rough ER.

🧬 The protein-making assembly line

This flow is gold for exam essays. Memorize it:

🏛️ Nucleus → ⚙️ Ribosome on Rough ER → 📦 Vesicle → 📮 Golgi → 📦 Vesicle → 🛡️ Plasma membrane → 🌍 Out!

Animal vs. plant vs. fungi 🐘🌳🍄

All eukaryotes, but with three distinct twists.

Animal 🐘 Fungi 🍄 Plant 🌳
Cell wall ❌ None ✅ Made of chitin ✅ Made of cellulose
Vacuoles Small, temporary Large central (support) Large central (support)
Chloroplasts ❌ None ❌ None ✅ Yes — photosynthesis!
Centrioles, cilia, flagella ✅ Yes ❌ Usually not (except some sex cells) ❌ Usually not (except some sex cells)

🧠 Remember it as:

Plants = cellulose wall + chloroplasts + big vacuole. Fungi = chitin wall + big vacuole, no chloroplasts. Animals = no wall + moving parts (centrioles/cilia).

Membrane structure 🧱

Now switching topics: the FLUID MOSAIC MODEL. A wobbly sandwich full of proteins.

Phospholipids — the bricks

A phospholipid is amphipathic — it has BOTH a polar and nonpolar end:

One phospholipid = polar head + 2 nonpolar fatty acid tails

When you drop them in water, they self-assemble into a bilayer — heads face out (toward water), tails hide in the middle.

The fluid mosaic model 🎨

The membrane is fluid (things drift around like a liquid) and mosaic (a mix of different molecules).

The fluid mosaic — you should be able to draw this. Include: phospholipids, integral & peripheral proteins, cholesterol, glycoprotein.

Membrane proteins — remember "CRATER"

CRATER
Connect (intercellular joinings)
Recognize (cell-cell recognition)
Anchor (attachment/anchorage)
Transport (active or passive)
Enzymes (catalyze reactions)
Receptors (signal transduction)

Integral vs. peripheral

Integral proteins

Embedded in the bilayer — often go all the way through. Transport channels, pumps, receptors.

Peripheral proteins

Attached to the surface only. Support & signaling roles.

Glycoproteins & glycolipids 🎗️

These are proteins/lipids with sugar chains sticking OUT of the cell. They act as ID tags — your immune system reads them to tell "self" from "invader." Blood types (A, B, O) work this way!

Transport across membranes 🚦

Four ways stuff crosses the membrane. Two need energy, two don't.

First: who can just… walk through?

The middle of the membrane is oily (nonpolar). So:

  • ✅ Small AND nonpolar = passes freely (O₂, CO₂)
  • ❌ Large, polar, or charged = blocked (glucose, Na⁺, Cl⁻)

Method 1: Simple diffusion

Simple diffusion = passive movement from high → low concentration, straight through the lipid bilayer. Only for small nonpolar molecules like O₂ and CO₂. No protein, no energy.

Method 2: Facilitated diffusion (channel proteins)

Facilitated diffusion = passive movement from high → low concentration, but through a channel protein. Used by ions and glucose. No energy needed, but the protein makes it selective.

Gated channels can open/close in response to voltage, light, or a chemical binding. This is how nerves work.

Method 3: Active transport (pump proteins)

Active transport = pushing molecules from LOW → HIGH concentration (uphill!) using a pump protein powered by ATP.

Pushing a ball uphill — requires energy!

Method 4: Osmosis

Special enough to get its own section 👇

🧠 Quick summary

MethodDirectionProtein?Energy?
Simple diffusionDown gradientNoNo
Facilitated diffusionDown gradientYes (channel)No
OsmosisWater only, down its own gradientYes (aquaporin)No
Active transportUp gradientYes (pump)Yes (ATP)

B2.1.5

Osmosis 💧

Everyone gets confused. Here's the fix.

Osmosis = the passive movement of water through a membrane, from an area of low solute to high solute concentration. Water uses a channel protein called an aquaporin to get through.

💡 The magic phrase

Solute sucks. 💥

The side with more solute pulls water toward it. Think of it as a tug-of-war — whoever has more solute wins the water.

The tonicity vocabulary

TermMeaningWhat happens to a cell placed in it?
Hypertonic Stronger solution (more solute outside) Water leaves cell → cell shrinks (crenation / plasmolysis)
Hypotonic Weaker solution (less solute outside) Water enters cell → cell swells or pops (lysis)
Isotonic Same concentration on both sides No net movement — cell stays normal

⚠️ Watch out — mixing up hyper and hypo

Hyper = high (hypertension = high pressure). Hypertonic = HIGH solute. Water leaves the cell.
Hypo = low. Hypotonic = LOW solute. Water enters the cell.

Compartmentalization 🏘️

Why bother having all those organelles? Here's the answer.

Advantages of having compartments

⚡

Efficient metabolism

Enzymes and substrates can be concentrated together in one small space.

🧪

Local conditions

Different pH, ion concentrations, etc. can be maintained in different organelles.

☠️

Toxic isolation

Dangerous things (digestive enzymes in lysosomes) are locked away so they don't destroy the cell.

🔀

Flexibility

Cell can adjust numbers of organelles based on need (e.g. more mitochondria = more energy).

Why separate the nucleus from the cytoplasm?

Because it lets eukaryotes do post-transcriptional modification — the mRNA can be edited AFTER being copied from DNA but BEFORE it reaches ribosomes. Prokaryotes can't do this; their ribosomes start translating while mRNA is still being made.

DNA → transcription → mRNA → processing → edited mRNA → translation → Protein
B2.3.1 – B2.3.4

Stem cells & differentiation 🌱

How one fertilized egg becomes 200+ cell types.

What is a stem cell?

A cell with two superpowers:

  1. Can divide endlessly to make more copies of itself.
  2. Can differentiate into specialized cell types.

The story

  1. Sperm + egg = zygote (a single stem cell)
  2. Zygote divides many times → blastocyst (hollow ball with inner cell mass)
  3. Stem cells detect morphogens — signal molecules whose concentration tells them what to become
  4. They differentiate by turning some genes on and others off
  5. Result: muscle cell, neuron, red blood cell, skin cell, etc.

🎯 Morphogens

These are chemicals released from a source cell. Cells CLOSE to the source detect high concentrations; cells FAR from the source detect low concentrations. So the same signal creates different tissues in a gradient across the embryo!

Types of stem cells — the potency ladder

TypeCan become…Example
Totipotent Anything — including placenta cells Zygote (days 0–4)
Pluripotent Any body cell (but NOT placenta) Inner cell mass of blastocyst
Multipotent A few related cell types Bone marrow → any blood cell
Unipotent One cell type only Skin (epidermal) stem cells

Stem cell niches in adults

Even adults have some stem cells left over. They live in special microenvironments called niches, where niche cells keep them in the right conditions to divide & differentiate when needed:

🦴 Bone marrow niche

Hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) make new blood cells (red, white, platelets). This is hematopoiesis.

💇 Hair follicle niche

Located in a bulge in the follicle. Multipotent — can regenerate skin & hair tissues. Huge therapeutic potential.

Surface area to volume ratio 📐

The single reason cells are so small.

Everything the cell needs (O₂, glucose) has to cross the membrane. So surface area = how fast stuff can enter/leave.

Meanwhile, volume = how much stuff the cell needs. Bigger cell = more demand.

The problem

When a cell gets bigger, volume grows faster than surface area. Imagine a cube:

SideSA (6s²)Vol (s³)SA:Vol
1616 : 1 😄
22483 : 1
496641.5 : 1
1060010000.6 : 1 😰

See? A cell that gets too big literally can't absorb enough nutrients to feed itself. This is why cells stay small — and if they need more area, they either:

  • Stay small and divide
  • Grow special shapes (long & thin like neurons, or covered in microvilli like intestinal cells)

🧠 One-line takeaway

Big cells starve. That's why we're multicellular, and why intestinal cells have those little finger-like microvilli.

Final prep

Exam-day tips 🎯

  • Draw big. Small drawings lose marks for clarity. Fill the space.
  • Label with lines that touch the structure. A label line ending in empty space = no mark.
  • Give function AND structure. If asked to "annotate," you MUST include what the structure does.
  • Use IB command terms carefully. "Outline" = short list. "Explain" = give reasons. "Compare" = similarities AND differences.
  • Check units in magnification questions. Convert everything to the same unit BEFORE dividing.
  • Watch for the trick word "all." "All cells have ribosomes" ✅. "All cells have mitochondria" ❌ (prokaryotes don't).
  • Memorize these lists exactly: the 4 features common to all cells, MR H GREN, CRATER, the potency ladder.

⚠️ Classic exam traps

  • Confusing hyper/hypotonic → remember "solute sucks"
  • Saying prokaryotes have a nucleus (they don't — just a nucleoid REGION)
  • Forgetting active transport needs ATP
  • Writing "diffuses through channel protein" for O₂ — that's simple diffusion, no protein needed!
  • Confusing rough ER (proteins) with smooth ER (lipids)
Test yourself

Self-quiz 🎓

Click each question to reveal the answer. No cheating!

1. What are the three statements of cell theory?
Answer: (1) All living things are made of cells. (2) The cell is the basic unit of life. (3) All cells come from pre-existing cells.
2. Name the four features common to ALL cells.
Answer: DNA, cytoplasm, ribosomes, plasma membrane.
3. Formula for magnification?
Answer: Magnification = drawing size ÷ actual size. Always convert units first!
4. Give one advantage and one disadvantage of the electron microscope vs. light microscope.
Advantage: Much higher magnification & resolution. Disadvantage: Sample must be dead; images are only in black & white; very expensive.
5. Prokaryote DNA vs. eukaryote DNA — three differences?
Prokaryote: naked (no histones), circular/looped, in nucleoid region.
Eukaryote: bound to histones, linear, inside nucleus.
6. What does the rough ER do and how is it different from smooth ER?
Rough ER: covered in ribosomes; makes proteins for export.
Smooth ER: no ribosomes; makes lipids and detoxifies drugs.
7. What are the 8 functions of life (MR H GREN)?
Metabolism, Response, Homeostasis, Growth, Reproduction, Excretion, Nutrition — plus Movement.
8. What is the cell wall of plants made of? Fungi?
Plants: cellulose. Fungi: chitin.
9. Why do red blood cells not have a nucleus?
Answer: A nucleus (~10 μm) is too big to fit through capillaries (~8 μm). RBCs eject it during development so they can squeeze through.
10. What does "amphipathic" mean and why is it important?
Answer: Having both hydrophilic (water-loving) and hydrophobic (water-hating) parts. Phospholipids are amphipathic — that's why they spontaneously form bilayers in water.
11. Name 4 kinds of molecules that CAN'T cross the membrane by simple diffusion, and why.
Answer: Large molecules (like glucose), polar molecules (like water — needs aquaporin), and charged ions (Na⁺, Cl⁻). They can't get through the nonpolar hydrocarbon core.
12. Give the CRATER functions of membrane proteins.
Connect · Recognize · Anchor · Transport · Enzymes · Receptors.
13. What's the difference between facilitated diffusion and active transport?
Facilitated diffusion: passive, moves DOWN concentration gradient through a channel protein, no ATP.
Active transport: moves UP concentration gradient through a pump protein, requires ATP.
14. Define osmosis.
Answer: The passive movement of water molecules through a selectively permeable membrane, from an area of low solute concentration to an area of high solute concentration (via aquaporins).
15. If a red blood cell is placed in pure water, what happens?
Answer: Pure water is hypotonic to the cell. Water rushes IN by osmosis. RBC swells and eventually bursts (lysis).
16. Give 3 advantages of compartmentalization in eukaryotic cells.
Answer: (1) Enzymes and substrates concentrated → more efficient. (2) Local conditions like pH can be optimized. (3) Toxic/damaging substances (e.g. digestive enzymes) can be isolated in specific organelles (lysosomes).
17. What's the difference between totipotent, pluripotent, and multipotent stem cells?
Totipotent: can become ANY cell, including placenta. Only in very early embryo.
Pluripotent: can become any body cell (not placenta). Found in inner cell mass of blastocyst.
Multipotent: can become a few related types. Bone marrow (blood cells).
18. Why do cells stay small?
Answer: As a cell gets bigger, volume increases faster than surface area. Eventually SA:Vol becomes too small for enough materials to enter/leave. So cells either stay small, divide, or develop shapes like microvilli that increase surface area.
19. What are morphogens and how do they work?
Answer: Signaling chemicals secreted by source cells. They form a concentration gradient — nearby cells detect high concentrations, distant cells detect low. Different concentrations trigger different genes to switch on, causing cells to differentiate into different tissue types.
20. Name two stem cell niches in adult humans and what they do.
Bone marrow: hematopoietic stem cells → all types of blood cells.
Hair follicle bulge: multipotent stem cells → regenerate skin and hair tissues.
